### Ready-to-paste abstract

Years after a talk called "Linux is a cult, but a good cult," the metaphor has become unexpectedly useful: the machines have joined the cult, while the humans remain free to leave. An Osborne 1 becomes a bus; people and software take turns at the wheel. The jokes are the entrance to serious questions about who granted authority, what a tool can actually do, and what evidence survives the performance.

We move from capability to role-fit, from tool enthusiasm to provable control, and from impressive outputs to credit and provenance. The "Skynet tax" asks four practical questions about any apparently autonomous system: What authority did it receive? What consequential resources could it touch? How could it fail? Who could stop it? The talk also asks who made the work, whose language and ideas were carried forward, and who gets to walk away.

Part Linux-culture retrospective, part playful technology field guide, this session offers a human-sized exit test: enjoy what you like; give people credit; don't insist everybody join; and make the brakes visible.

### Key takeaways

- Judge tools by role fit and explicit authority, not raw capability.
- Explain AI incidents with resources, permissions, failure modes and stop controls.
- Provenance and the right to exit remain human and community responsibilities.

### Ready-to-paste alternate abstract

Open-source communities are good at inspecting what a tool does, not just how impressive a demo looks. This talk uses a comic Linux-community metaphor to teach four repeatable checks for AI-assisted workflows: name the authority, bound the resources, publish a failure path and record who can stop the system. A short tour of real, sourced handoff experiments shows the difference between a model producing convincing text and a system having permission to act. Technical controls, source credit and a human exit path matter more than a theatrical benchmark.
